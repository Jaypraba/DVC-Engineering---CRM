Every contractor in London offers a free quotation. The visit takes forty
minutes, one person attends, and a number arrives by email a fortnight later.
The number is a bid. It is written to win the job, and everything uncomfortable
about the building is left out of it, because saying it would lose the work.

That visit is not free. Its cost sits inside the price of every job that firm
wins, paid for by clients who did appoint them. And it is expensive in a second
way, which is that nobody on that visit was in a position to tell you anything
you did not want to hear.

## What a paid visit buys

Three people, at the same time, on site together. An interior designer, a
structural engineer and a contractor. They disagree with each other in front of
you, which is the point. The engineer says a thing is possible; the contractor
says what it costs to build; the designer asks whether the room it produces is
worth having.

Then a written report within five working days, with a cost band we are willing
to be held to and a recommended route forward — including, sometimes, that you
should not do the project, or should not do it with us.

## The uncomfortable arithmetic

On a £900,000 project, the difference between the right structural strategy and
the wrong one is routinely six figures. It is decided in the first fortnight,
usually before anyone has been paid anything. Deciding it on the basis of free
advice from someone who needs the job is the most expensive economy in
residential construction.

We would rather charge for two hours and be honest in them.
